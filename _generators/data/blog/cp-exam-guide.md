If a C&P exam is coming up, you are probably nervous. For many of us this one appointment feels like the whole claim rides on it, and not knowing is the worst part. This guide covers what a C&P exam is, how to prepare, what to do in the room and what happens after.

## What a C&P exam is

C&P stands for Compensation and Pension. The VA sends you to a provider who documents your condition. The examiner does not decide your claim and does not approve or deny you. The examiner fills out a form called a DBQ, a Disability Benefits Questionnaire, and documents what they observe. A rater at the regional office decides your claim, using that DBQ plus all the evidence in your file.

So the exam is one piece of the claim. A short exam is not automatically a bad exam. A cold examiner is not automatically a bad outcome. Bedside manner has nothing to do with your rating. If you walk out feeling it went fast or felt impersonal, that happens all the time and the claim still comes back fine.

## Before the exam

Preparation is mostly getting your own story straight.

Know what you claimed and why. Review which conditions you are being seen for. Examiners sometimes cover several in one visit, and you do not want to forget one because you got flustered.

Think through your worst days, not only today. Many vets walk in on a decent day and describe how they feel right then. Your condition is also the flare-ups, the bad weeks, the days you could not work or get out of bed or be around people. The VA rates the full picture, including your worst, so go in ready to describe the whole range.

Know what the VA measures. The VA rates every condition against specific criteria written into the rating schedule in 38 CFR. An exact list separates a 30 percent rating from a 50 or a 70, and it is different for every condition. If you know those criteria going in, you know what the examiner is documenting and which parts of your daily reality matter to your rating. VA Ready includes the rating criteria and filing guides, pulled straight from the regs, condition by condition. Whether you use the app or read 38 CFR yourself, do not go in blind to how you are being measured.

Write a few notes. A short list keeps you from blanking: when it started, how often it happens, what you can no longer do, how it affects your work, sleep and relationships. Nerves make people forget things.

Bring your evidence if you have it. Records, a symptom log, statements, anything that backs up your claim. If you keep a daily symptom log, this is what it is for.

Show up. Missing a C&P exam can sink a claim faster than almost anything. If you cannot make it, reschedule through the proper channel. Do not no-show.

## During the exam

Be honest and thorough, and do not tough it out.

Many of us were trained to minimize: suck it up, walk it off, never admit weakness. In a C&P exam that instinct hurts you. When the examiner asks how you are doing, the complete answer is the right answer. Do not downplay your pain, do not say you are fine when you are not, and do not gloss over the bad days because you do not want to look weak. Telling the examiner the full truth about how this affects you is the point of being there.

Do not exaggerate or invent anything either. You do not need to, and it can blow up your credibility. Do not perform. Stop minimizing and tell the complete truth, including the parts we are trained to hide.

Answer the question they ask, but if something important is not getting covered, say so: "Can I add something about how this affects my sleep?" You are allowed to make sure the full picture gets on paper.

If a movement hurts, say so. If the examiner asks you to bend or reach and it causes pain, tell them. They need that to document it.

Take your notes in with you. Nobody thinks less of you for being prepared.

## After the exam

You probably will not get an answer in the room. The examiner finishes the DBQ and sends it back, your claim goes to the rater, and the decision comes later. Do not read the outcome from how the exam felt. Fast and cold can come back favorable. Long and friendly can come back lower than you hoped.

You can request a copy of your C&P exam results. You are entitled to your own records, and an accredited VSO can often help you get and read them.

If the rating comes back lower than it should, you can challenge it. You can file a Supplemental Claim with new evidence or a Higher-Level Review, or appeal to the Board of Veterans' Appeals. In fiscal year 2024 the Board decided 71,262 appeals under the current system: 38.4 percent were allowed with some relief and no remand, 28.4 percent were sent back for more work, and 16.0 percent were denied (Board of Veterans' Appeals Annual Report, FY 2024). One exam is not the final word.

## The stress of waiting

Waiting on a decision is one of the hardest parts of this process, and few people talk about it. If you are losing sleep over it, you are not weak and you are not alone. Some things help: work with an accredited VSO so you are not navigating it by yourself, lean on the people in your life, and remember that filing and showing up was the hard part. The wait is out of your hands now.

If the stress is pulling you somewhere dark, reach out to someone. The Veterans Crisis Line is there 24/7. Dial 988, then press 1. Your buddies are there, your people are there. You made it through worse than a claim decision. Do not carry the wait alone.

## The short version

A C&P exam is one documented piece of your claim. Prepare by getting your story straight and thinking through your worst days, not only today. In the room, put down the instinct to minimize and tell the complete, honest truth. Afterward, do not judge the result by how the exam felt, and know that a low rating can be challenged. Work with an accredited VSO and do not let the wait eat at you.
