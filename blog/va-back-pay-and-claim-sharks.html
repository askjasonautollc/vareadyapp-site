Follow the Money: Why Predators Fixate on Your VA Back Pay

Search "VA back pay calculator" and you will drown in law firms and lead-gen sites. There is a reason they all crowd around back pay, and once you see it, the whole playbook makes sense.

Jason, CW2 (Ret), 131A · August 3, 2026 · 5 min read
VA READY

Type "VA back pay calculator" into a search bar and look at what comes back. Law firms. Lead-generation sites. "Free evaluation" forms that want your name, your email, your phone number, and your rating before they show you a single number.

That is not an accident. Back pay is the one place a fee can legally come from, so it is the one place fee-seekers gather. Once you understand that, you can walk right past the hook.

Follow the money

When the VA grants or increases a rating, it usually owes you a lump sum for the time between your effective date and the day the decision landed. That is your back pay, also called retroactive pay or "retro." It is a one-time check, and it can be large.

Here is the part most veterans are never told. Under federal law, an accredited attorney or claims agent can only charge a fee that is a percentage of your past-due (retroactive) benefits. Fees at or under 20 percent of that retro are presumed reasonable, and fees over 33 and a third percent are presumed unreasonable (38 CFR 14.636(e)). The VA even withholds that cut directly out of the retro lump sum and pays the representative before you ever see it (14.636(h)).

Read that again. The fee is a slice of your back pay. It is not a slice of your monthly check going forward. It is not a flat rate. It is a percentage of that one big retroactive number.

So if you were building a business around VA-claim fees, where would you point every ad, every calculator, every "see what you are owed" button? At the back pay. That is the entire prize.

The rules nobody puts in the ad

Three facts cut through most of the noise:

  1. No one can legally charge you to file your original claim. Not an attorney, not a claims agent, and absolutely not an unaccredited "coach." Under 38 CFR 14.636(c), a fee can only be charged after the VA has issued its first decision on the claim. Charging you just to fill out and file the initial claim is not allowed.
  2. Only VA-accredited people can represent you for a fee. The VA accredits attorneys, claims agents, and Veterans Service Organization (VSO) representatives under 38 USC 5901. Everyone else is operating outside the system.
  3. VSO help is free. All of it. A VA-accredited VSO can prepare and file your claim, including your very first one, at no cost. The VA says so plainly on its own find-a-representative page.

Where it turns predatory

The gap between those rules and what actually happens is where veterans get hurt.

In 2023, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the VA jointly warned that unaccredited individuals and organizations were aggressively soliciting veterans and pushing them to sign contracts for a percentage of their retroactive or future benefits in exchange for help preparing initial claims, which is help that cannot legally be charged for. The agencies pointed to veterans paying substantial illegal fees. These outfits often call themselves "coaches" or "consultants" precisely because they are not accredited.

A common pricing model reported across veteran journalism is a fee equal to several times your first month of increased pay, collected up front, for help you could have gotten free. It is dressed up as a service. It is a cut of your back pay by another name.

States have started swinging back, with attorneys general in several states reaching settlements with unaccredited claim consultants over fees charged to veterans. In Congress, the GUARD VA Benefits Act (H.R. 1732, 119th Congress) would restore criminal penalties for unaccredited actors who charge illegal fees, penalties that were stripped from the law about twenty years ago. As of this writing it sits in committee, bipartisan but not yet passed.

The red flags, in one place

  • Someone wants a percentage of your back pay to file your original claim.
  • They cannot show you a VA accreditation number. You can verify one yourself with the VA's accreditation search.
  • They "guarantee" a rating or a dollar amount. Only the VA decides ratings.
  • The calculator will not show you a number until you hand over your contact information.

Any one of those is your cue to slow down.

An honest number has no hook

A back pay estimate is genuinely useful. Knowing roughly what a decision could be worth helps you plan, budget, and decide whether to keep pushing. The problem was never the calculator. The problem is what most of them are attached to.

An honest calculator does one thing: it shows you the number. No contact form. No fee agreement. No "our attorneys will be in touch." Just the math, and a clear pointer to free, accredited help if you want a human in your corner.
