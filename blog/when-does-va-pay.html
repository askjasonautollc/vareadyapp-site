When the VA pays you: effective dates, Intent to File and back pay

How your effective date, an Intent to File and back pay set when VA money starts, and why your first check lands when it does.

Jason, CW2 (Ret), 131A · June 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Your claim got approved. Now you are looking at your decision letter with one question: when do I get paid, and how much?

Almost nobody explains the timing plainly. When my own claim came back, I did not understand how it worked, and I was confused when the money did not show up when I expected it. This is the walk-through I wanted.

Every claim is different. Your dates depend on your effective date, your decision date and your situation. Use this to understand how the system works, then confirm your specifics on VA.gov or with your accredited VSO.

Four dates that get mixed up

Several dates sound alike and mean different things.

The decision date is the day the VA approved your claim. It feels like the important date, but for payment purposes it mostly is not.

The effective date is when your entitlement to compensation begins, and it is the most important date for your money. In most cases the VA uses the date it received your claim. If you filed within one year of leaving active duty, the effective date may be the day after your separation, which can mean a lot of back pay.

Your pay start date is not the day of your effective date. The VA does not pay for partial months. Payments begin the first day of the month following your effective date.

When the money lands depends on processing. The VA pays monthly going forward, and back pay arrives as a separate lump sum.

Intent to File

Many veterans do not know about Intent to File. Before you file your full claim, you can submit an Intent to File, which locks in your effective date while you gather what you need.

An Intent to File is a notice to the VA that you plan to submit a claim. It can protect months of retroactive compensation. Under 38 CFR 3.155, if the VA receives your complete claim within one year of your Intent to File, it treats the claim as filed on the date the Intent to File arrived, not the later date you finish and submit.

Say you file an Intent to File on June 1, spend six months gathering medical records and a nexus letter, and submit your full claim on December 1. Your benefits are backdated to June 1. Without the Intent to File, that same December 1 claim would have a December 1 effective date, and you would lose six months of pay.

The deadline is firm. If you do not submit your completed claim within one year of your Intent to File, you lose the earlier date, and the VA uses the date it receives your actual claim, which can wipe out months of back pay. You cannot extend the year. If you need more time, you file a new Intent to File, and your date starts over from the new one.

You can file an Intent to File online at VA.gov, by phone at 800-827-1000, or by mail using VA Form 21-0966. When you start a disability claim online at VA.gov, the system saves your Intent to File automatically when you begin, even if you do not finish right away. Keep your confirmation. That is your proof of effective date.

An Intent to File is not a claim. It only protects your date, and you still have to file the actual claim within the year.

How payment timing works

VA disability pay is not paid for partial months, and no payment is made for the month in which your claim was received, even if your effective date falls in that month. Your entitlement starts on the first day of the month following your effective date.

My own claim shows how it plays out. It was approved on February 5. My pay start date was March 1. My first full payment did not hit my account until April 1.

Here is why:

  • My effective date was in early February. The VA does not pay for the month the claim falls in, so February did not pay.
  • Entitlement started the first full month after the effective date, which is March 1.
  • The VA issues payment going forward, so my first regular monthly deposit landed at the start of April.

Nothing was wrong. The system is designed that way. I did not understand the timing, so it felt like something was off.

Back pay

Many veterans expect a large retroactive lump sum and are surprised when they do not get one. Back pay is not a bonus, and it is not automatically large. It depends on how far in the past your effective date sits.

Back pay, also called retroactive pay, is the compensation the VA owes you for the stretch between your effective date and the date it grants your claim. The VA pays it automatically once your claim is approved, usually as a lump sum by direct deposit. The two terms mean the same thing, and the VA uses them interchangeably.

What that means for the amount:

  • If the VA takes a year to decide, your effective date is a year in the past, and the VA owes you a year of back pay. That is the big lump sum people talk about.
  • If your claim is approved quickly, close to your effective date, there is little or no gap, so there is little or no back pay.

In my case, approval and effective date were close together. There was barely any retroactive period, so there was barely any back pay. My claim moved fast. The veterans who get huge back pay checks are usually the ones who waited a long time for a decision, or who locked in an early effective date through an Intent to File or a filing within one year of separation.

If you expect a lump sum and do not see much, check how far apart your effective date and decision date are. If they are close, that is your answer.

How back pay is calculated

The VA calculates back pay month by month, using the compensation rate that applied during each month you were owed. Because the VA updates its rates periodically for cost-of-living adjustments, older months in your back pay may be paid at the older rates in effect then. Your amount also reflects your combined rating and any dependents, and if your rating changed over the period, the back pay reflects the different tiers across those months.

How soon you see the money

VA.gov says that if your decision notice shows at least a 10 percent rating, you will get your first payment within 15 days. VA does not publish a timeline for the back pay lump sum, so treat that as the only firm number. Watch your bank for a deposit labeled something like VA Benefit or U.S. Treasury 310, and watch your mail for a separate statement breaking down your effective date, the monthly amounts and the total. Your regular monthly payments then continue on the first of each following month.

If it has been more than a few weeks past your decision and you have seen nothing, follow up. Something may be held up in processing, like your direct deposit details or a dependency verification. Keep your information current to avoid delays.

The quick version

  • Your effective date drives your money, not your decision date.
  • An Intent to File locks in your effective date for up to twelve months while you gather evidence. It is free, fast, and can protect months of back pay.
  • The VA does not pay for partial months, and not for the month your claim was received. Pay starts the first of the month after your effective date.
  • Back pay is the gap between your effective date and your approval. The bigger the gap, the bigger the back pay. A fast approval close to your effective date means little or none, and that is normal.
  • Back pay is calculated month by month at the rates in effect then, and usually arrives as a lump sum within a few weeks of approval.

What to do next

Your effective date controls your money, not the day you were approved. The best way to protect it is to file an Intent to File the moment you decide to pursue a claim, even before your evidence is together.

Find your effective date on your decision letter. Count to the first of the next month for when pay starts. Check the gap between your effective date and decision date to know whether back pay is coming.

If your numbers do not add up after a few weeks, get with your accredited VSO. They can pull your award details and tell you where your payment stands.
