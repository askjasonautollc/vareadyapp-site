Denied for "lack of continuity of symptoms"? How to bridge the gap

A lack-of-continuity denial points to a gap between service and diagnosis. Close it with a nexus opinion, buddy statements and secondary claims.

VA Ready App · June 15, 2026 · 5 min read

A denial for "lack of continuity of symptoms" is one of the most common denials the VA issues, and one of the most beatable. It closes one step. It does not close your claim. This article covers what the denial means and what to do about it.

What the denial means

The VA is saying it can see something happened in service and it can see you have the condition now, but it sees no thread connecting the two. The injury or event is in your record, the diagnosis is in your present, and nothing ties them together.

Many of us have that gap because we were trained never to go to medical. Going on sick call was weakness. Mission first, walk it off, don't be the guy who can't hang. So we never built the paper trail the VA wants to see. The same culture that kept you quiet in uniform made the hole in your file. That is not your fault, but fixing it is your job now, and it can be fixed.

Bridge the gap, don't prove a new injury

Usually you are not trying to prove a new injury. You are building a link between the service event and the condition you have today. There are several ways to build it, and you can use more than one at the same time.

The nexus

The connection between service and your current condition is called a nexus. Most often it is a medical opinion from a provider, VA or private, stating that your condition is "at least as likely as not" related to your service. The VA standard is not "for certain." At least as likely as not is basically 50/50. You do not have to prove it beyond a doubt. You have to tip it to even.

If you were diagnosed at your C&P exam, that is the VA's own doctor putting your condition on paper, and it works in your favor. Do not overlook evidence the VA already created for you.

Buddy statements

If your medical file is thin because you never went to sick call, statements fill it. You can prove a case with more than records.

A buddy statement (the VA calls it lay evidence) is a written account from someone who saw what happened or saw you struggle. That can be you, describing the event and how it has affected you since. It can be people you served with who remember the injury, the blast or the range time. It can be your spouse or family, who watched you come home different and live with it for years. "He stopped sleeping after that deployment." "He's had the ringing in his ears since he ran the range." "His back's been bad since we got out and he never said a word." Those count. They fill the gap the military left in your file.

Secondary connections

Some conditions do not connect straight back to service. They connect to another condition that does. Depression and anxiety often follow PTSD or living with chronic pain. Stomach issues like IBS frequently tie back to PTSD. If the direct route to service is hard to prove, the secondary route may be open. Map which of your conditions might link to each other, not only to service.

Noise exposure

Time around weapons is an exposure history whether or not it is written down: range time, combat arms, firearms instruction, the gun line, the flight line, the motor pool. Sustained noise exposure is a well understood path to hearing loss and tinnitus. That you lived it is part of your case.

Your options after a denial

After a denial, the usual paths are a Supplemental Claim or a Higher-Level Review. Do not refile the same claim and hope for a different answer. A Supplemental Claim works when you have new and relevant evidence to add, which is what nexus opinions and buddy statements are. Which path fits depends on your specifics, and that is where you bring in help.

Get an accredited VSO. They are free, by law, and they will sit down and build the strategy with you. After a denial the sharks come out. You will see ads promising to "maximize your rating" for a percentage of your back pay or a few thousand dollars up front. Some of them are not accredited, which means it is against federal law for them to charge you for claims help at all. They go after you at the moment you are denied and willing to try anything. An accredited VSO does the same work for nothing. Your back pay belongs to your family, not to a call center.

Also get current diagnoses on the record, VA or private. Build the medical record you were never allowed to build in uniform.

Check that your evidence is real

Your evidence has to hold up when a rater checks it. A nexus opinion, a study and a citation all have to. Some tools now generate a confident-sounding claim or a medical citation in seconds. If that citation does not exist, or does not say what the tool claims, you find out when your claim comes back denied again.

When you reference medical research, use real, published, peer-reviewed studies you can point to. Made-up evidence is worse than none, because it costs you credibility you cannot get back.

The short version

A "lack of continuity" denial means there is a gap. It does not mean you have no case. Close the gap with a nexus opinion, buddy statements, secondary connections and the evidence the VA put in your file at your C&P. Then file the right kind of review with that new evidence, with an accredited VSO helping.
