If you take medication for a service-connected condition, you may have seen something alarming in February 2026. The VA published a rule that told examiners to rate you on how you function while you are medicated. Then, ten days later, the VA pulled that rule back.

Both things really happened, and both are in the Federal Register. A lot of vets saw the first one and never saw the second, which is a bad combination. Here is the full sequence, with citations, and what the rules on the books actually say right now.

This is an explainer, not advice. Nothing here tells you what to do about your claim.

## What the February 17 rule said

On February 17, 2026, the VA published an interim final rule called "Evaluative Rating: Impact of Medication," at [91 FR 7118](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/17/2026-03068/evaluative-rating-impact-of-medication). An interim final rule takes effect right away instead of going through public comment first. This one was effective the day it published, with comments due 60 days later.

It amended one regulation: 38 CFR 4.10, the section titled "Functional impairment." The rule added two sentences:

"To ensure that disability evaluations are based on the actual level of functional impairment under the ordinary conditions of daily life, the medical examiner will not estimate or discount improvements to the disability due to the effects of medication or treatment, whether or not medication or treatment is included within specific rating criteria. If medication or other treatment lowers the level of disability, the rating will be based on that lowered disability level."

Read that second sentence again. Under that language, if your medication was working, the rating would be built on the medicated version of you, and a well-controlled condition looks less severe on paper. That is how a lot of vets read it, and on the face of the text it was a fair reading.

## Why the VA said it did that

The VA's own preamble is direct about the reason. It pointed to a court decision, *Ingram v. Collins*, which the VA cited as 38 Vet. App. 130 (2025). That case was decided by the U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims on March 12, 2025, in docket No. 23-1798.

In *Ingram*, the court held that "because the applicable diagnostic codes (DCs) and special musculoskeletal regulations do not reference medication, the Board must discount beneficial medication effects when assigning an evaluation." In plain terms, for those musculoskeletal conditions, the Board was supposed to look past the medication and consider how bad the condition would be without it.

The VA did not like that. In the interim final rule the VA wrote that it was amending the text "to correct judicial interpretations that VA has concluded misconstrue the role of medication and treatment in evaluating functional impairment." The VA said *Ingram* "could be applied broadly to over 500 separate diagnostic codes, requiring re-adjudications of over 350,000 currently pending claims."

That is the VA's characterization of the case, not the court's. Keep the two separate: a court held one thing, and an agency published a regulation meant to override that holding going forward. Those are different kinds of authority.

## What the VA said when it pulled the rule

On February 27, 2026, the VA published "Rescission of Interim Final Rule, Evaluative Rating: Impact of Medication," at [91 FR 9712](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/27/2026-03940/rescission-of-interim-final-rule-evaluative-rating-impact-of-medication), Docket No. VA-2026-VBA-0067. It was effective immediately on publication, and it republished 38 CFR 4.10 in its earlier form.

The VA gave a short explanation. It said it "issued the rule to clarify existing policy and protect veterans' benefits in the wake of an ongoing court action," and that it "recognizes that many commenters construed the interim final rule as something that could result in adverse consequences."

The rescission also included this line, which matters more than the rest:

"This action does not resolve the legal questions now before the courts; it simply restores prior regulatory text to maintain stability."

So the VA was not saying the courts got it right. It was saying it was putting the old text back and leaving the legal fight alone.

## What 38 CFR 4.10 says now

The rescission republished the section, so the operative text is the same one that existed before February 17. It reads, in relevant part, that "the basis of disability evaluations is the ability of the body as a whole, or of the psyche, or of a system or organ of the body to function under the ordinary conditions of daily life including employment."

The two medication sentences are gone. There is no language in 38 CFR 4.10 today directing examiners to rate you at your medicated level.

## The case law that governs

With the rule withdrawn, the controlling authority goes back to the courts.

The foundation is *Jones v. Shinseki*, 26 Vet. App. 56 (2012). Note the year: this is the 2012 decision, and it is frequently confused with a different 2010 case with the same name. In *Jones*, the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims held that when a diagnostic code's rating criteria do not contemplate the effects of medication, the Board errs by denying a higher rating based on the ameliorative effects of medication. The court's reasoning was that some diagnostic codes name medication and most do not, and that difference was a deliberate choice by the VA, so the Board cannot add medication as a criterion where the VA left it out.

*McCarroll v. McDonald*, 28 Vet. App. 267 (2016), decided en banc, is the follow-on. There the court found the *Jones* rule did not apply, because the diagnostic code at issue did contemplate medication. The VA's own preamble notes that *McCarroll* was the first decision to phrase the rule as requiring the Board "to discount the ameliorative effects of medication," and that *Jones* itself did not use the word "discount" in the rating context.

*Ingram* then applied that line of cases to musculoskeletal conditions, which is what triggered the February rule.

## What happened after the rescission

There is one more step, and it is the piece most February coverage missed.

The government had appealed *Ingram* to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, docket No. 2025-1972. On March 30, 2026, the Federal Circuit issued a [nonprecedential order](https://www.cafc.uscourts.gov/opinions-orders/25-1972.ORDER.3-30-2026_2668347.pdf) stating: "The parties having so agreed, it is ordered that: (1) The proceeding is DISMISSED under Fed. R. App. P. 42(b). (2) Each side shall bear their own costs." The mandate issued the same day.

Be precise about what that is. It is a voluntary dismissal the parties agreed to. The Federal Circuit did not rule on whether *Ingram* was correctly decided. It ended the appeal, which leaves the lower court's decision undisturbed. The National Veterans Legal Services Program, which represented Mr. Ingram, described the outcome as a victory establishing that the VA cannot assign a lower rating based on ameliorative medication effects. That is a party's characterization of the result, not language from the court's order.

So the honest summary is this. The February rule came and went in ten days, the old regulatory text is back, and the appeal that might have unsettled *Ingram* was dropped.

## Where the criteria do count medication

Here is the part that gets flattened in most write-ups. The *Jones* rule has never been absolute. It applies where the rating criteria are silent on medication. Plenty of criteria are not silent, and in those the medication is baked into the standard itself. Three verified examples from the rating schedule:

- **Mental disorders, 38 CFR 4.130.** The General Rating Formula sets the 10 percent level at "occupational and social impairment due to mild or transient symptoms which decrease work efficiency and ability to perform occupational tasks only during periods of significant stress, or symptoms controlled by continuous medication." Continuous medication is written directly into that level.

- **Asthma, diagnostic code 6602, 38 CFR 4.97.** Nearly every level names a treatment. The 10 percent level includes "intermittent inhalational or oral bronchodilator therapy." The 30 percent level includes "daily inhalational or oral bronchodilator therapy, or; inhalational anti-inflammatory medication." The 60 percent level includes "intermittent (at least three per year) courses of systemic (oral or parenteral) corticosteroids." The 100 percent level includes daily use of high dose systemic corticosteroids or immunosuppressive medications.

- **Hypertension, diagnostic code 7101, 38 CFR 4.104.** The 10 percent level includes the "minimum evaluation for an individual with a history of diastolic pressure predominantly 100 or more who requires continuous medication for control."

One note on the mental health criteria. The VA proposed a full rewrite of 38 CFR 4.130 back on February 15, 2022, at 87 FR 8498. As of this writing that remains a proposed rule and has not been finalized, so the formula quoted above is still the one in the regulation. A proposed rule is not law.

## What we could not confirm

A few things circulated in February that we could not verify from a primary source, so we are not stating them as fact:

- Reports of a public statement from the Secretary around February 19 saying the rule would not be enforced. We found news coverage of it but no primary document.
- Reports of a lawsuit filed against the interim final rule itself. The rescission refers to "an ongoing court action" and to "legal questions now before the courts," but the VA did not name the case, and we did not locate the filing.
- Whether any claims were actually decided under the interim final rule during the ten days it was in effect. We found no primary source either way.

As of this writing, the Federal Register shows only two documents under the rule's regulation identifier, RIN 2900-AS49: the February 17 interim final rule and the February 27 rescission. The VA has not published a replacement or a new proposal on this.

## The bottom line

A regulation that would have changed how medication factors into your rating was published on February 17, 2026 and withdrawn on February 27, 2026. The prior text of 38 CFR 4.10 is back in place. The medication question is governed by the courts, mainly *Jones v. Shinseki*, 26 Vet. App. 56 (2012), as applied in *McCarroll* and *Ingram*, and by the specific wording of whichever diagnostic code applies, because some of them name medication and most do not.

If you want to read it yourself rather than take anyone's summary, both Federal Register documents are linked above and the rating schedule is public at 38 CFR Part 4.
